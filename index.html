Tamim Ahmed, PhD

Experts make judgments they cannot fully explain. I build AI that learns those judgments and hands them back, sharper.

Dr. Tamim Ahmed is a Postdoctoral Research Associate and the lead for computation at the Human-Technology Interaction Lab of the USC Iovine and Young Academy, where he is also the instructor of record for IDSN 543: Augmented Intelligence. His work sits between clinicians, surgeons, and machine learning: he builds systems that learn how experts judge, explain what they see, and know when to hand a decision back to a person.

He earned his PhD in Biomedical Engineering from USC in 2026. His dissertation, A Probabilistic Computational Model for Explainable Augmented Intelligence in Clinical Practice, produced xAARA, an explainable movement-assessment system co-designed over thirty months with therapists at Shirley Ryan AbilityLab, the top-ranked rehabilitation hospital in the United States, where it is now in daily clinical use.

His method, a bidirectional translator between expert judgment and computation, is published in Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence and ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction. It now anchors an invited $800,000 Department of Veterans Affairs proposal on which he is Co-Investigator at 50% effort, a surgical wound-assessment program he leads with Johnson & Johnson MedTech and the Keck School of Medicine, and a computable model of how the Academy's own students learn.

He has spoken at Shirley Ryan AbilityLab's Research Seminar Series and led the Academy's HTI x IQ Night on forward deployed engineering. Before his doctorate he trained as an electrical engineer at BUET in Bangladesh, where he built medical-device prototypes, including the deep-learning ultrasound method behind his provisional patent.

HTI Lab · research outcomes

Three domains, one process.

Every system below was built the same way: embed with the experts, turn their tacit judgment into an explicit vocabulary and a data design, build the models on that data, validate against the experts, and hand the result back as something they use. Open a domain to see the system, the interfaces, and the results.

Co-designed standards

The expert vocabulary and the data design come first, with the people who will use the system.

Probabilistic translation

A translator carries the expert's judgment into the models and the models' evidence back to the expert, with calibrated uncertainty.

The expert decides

The system proposes, explains, and defers. The clinician, surgeon, or instructor keeps the decision.

Teaching

IDSN 543: Augmented Intelligence

Instructor of record, USC Iovine and Young Academy, since spring 2025. Designed from scratch and taught twice to students from art, design, business, and engineering.

The course teaches students to fit a model into a working pipeline across four cycles: image, time series, language, and video. Each cycle runs the way a real project runs. Students design the data structure, design the data collection process, engineer the features, and only then build the model. They audit it against data they collected and against real-world data, evaluate it in a fit memo, deploy it as a working pipeline, and defend it in front of the class. They use AI tools throughout and are graded on what those tools cannot do for them.

Students come from computer science and business, strong designers with little technical depth, and most can get working code from an AI tool before they can explain it. I tune pace and content to the depth the room actually has and grade what the tools cannot do.

Students have gone on to data-science internships and full-time jobs at major companies, and several have used what they built in the course to improve their own startups. In the 2025 Johnson & Johnson MedTech research sprint, ten students I mentored designed, built, and deployed the annotation portal now used in the surgical pilot.
